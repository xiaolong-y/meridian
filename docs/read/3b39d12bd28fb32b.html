RIKEN Demonstrates High-Fidelity Simultaneous Control of Silicon Spin Qubits Above 99.9% Threshold

Quantum Computing Report Mohamed Abdel-Kareem 1d quantum 622 words 4 min read

In a study published in Nature Physics, researchers from RIKEN, National Taiwan University, ETH Zurich, and TU Delft have demonstrated simultaneous high-fidelity single-qubit operations across a five-qubit silicon (28Si/SiGe) quantum dot array using a single, shared microwave control line.
Detailed in the paper (“Simultaneous high-fidelity single-qubit gates in a spin qubit array“, the team identified AC-Stark shifts as the main source of parallel drive crosstalk and introduced an N(N-1) pairwise calibration protocol that preserves gate fidelities above 99.9% during simultaneous 5-qubit operation.

Overcoming Parallel Drive Crosstalk in Silicon Spin Arrays
Because idle silicon spin qubits dephase significantly faster (T2* ≈ 10 μs) than driven qubits (T2,R ≈ 100 μs), running cyclic quantum error correction (QEC) protocols requires simultaneous multi-qubit operations. However, driving multiple spin qubits in parallel previously caused gate fidelities to degrade from >99.9% down to 99.5%.
The team identified off-resonant microwave-drive-induced AC-Stark shifts as the primary source of parallel gate errors. Because the resulting phase shifts (Δφij) sum linearly, the crosstalk can be compensated by measuring pairwise phase accumulation between individual qubit pairs, scaling as N(N-1) rather than 2N-N-1.
- Exceeding the 99.9% Fault-Tolerant Threshold: All five qubits maintained simultaneous single-qubit gate fidelities ≥99.9% during fully parallel five-qubit operation, satisfying the minimum requirements for 2D surface code QEC.
- Low Residual Exchange (Joff < 16.6 kHz): Decoupled CZ sequence measurements confirmed that residual exchange interactions between neighboring quantum dots remained below 16.6 kHz during single-qubit driving.
- Wiring Reduction for Scalable QPUs: Demonstrating high-fidelity parallel control using a single impedance-matched microwave transmission line reduces physical wiring density and thermal loads, supporting modular silicon spin-qubit unit cells of ~10 qubits per shared control line.
The researchers note that replacing the surface micromagnets with integrated back-end-of-line (BEOL) nanomagnets could double the local field gradient, enabling faster gate operations (2–3 MHz) at lower microwave power and opening a pathway toward 10-qubit parallel control modules in commercial silicon manufacturing.
What This Means (In Plain English)
To run quantum error correction on a silicon quantum computer, the processor must execute operations on many qubits at the exact same time. However, when qubits sit idle waiting for their turn, their delicate quantum states rapidly decay (dephase) within microseconds. Previously, turning on microwave signals to control multiple silicon spin qubits simultaneously caused severe interference (crosstalk), causing gate accuracy to drop sharply. Physicists at RIKEN discovered that this crosstalk is caused by “AC-Stark shifts”—a predictable shift in a qubit’s frequency caused by neighboring microwave pulses. By proving that these shifts add up linearly, the team developed a simple pairwise calibration trick to offset the frequency shifts. Using a single shared microwave control line, they operated up to three silicon qubits simultaneously at 99.99% accuracy, and all five qubits at ≥99.9% accuracy—crossing the fault-tolerant threshold needed for practical error-corrected quantum chips.
