Cross-Platform Cloud Benchmark Reveals Wide Gap Between Physical Qubit Counts and Entangled GHZ State Sizes

Quantum Computing Report Mohamed Abdel-Kareem 1d quantum 523 words 3 min read

A Spanish research consortium led by Gradiant, the University of the Basque Country (UPV/EHU), and the Basque Center for Applied Mathematics (BCAM), alongside partners from the University of Deusto, Mondragon Unibertsitatea, Ikerlan, Vicomtech, and Quantum Mads, has benchmarked nine cloud-accessible quantum processors across five hardware vendors using Greenberger-Horne-Zeilinger (GHZ) state generation. Executed through standard remote cloud workflows without error mitigation, the study shows that large superconducting processors maintain multipartite entanglement across less than a quarter of their physical registers.

Implementing the multipartite entanglement protocol from the QuSquare benchmark suite, the researchers used Direct Fidelity Estimation (DFE) across 2,952 randomly sampled stabilizers per circuit size to evaluate processors accessed via the IBM Quantum Platform, Amazon Braket, and the Galicia Supercomputing Center (CESGA). While previous single-vendor demonstrations have reported larger entangled states using custom pulse compilation, post-selection, and readout mitigation, this evaluation isolated out-of-the-box performance experienced by typical cloud users.
The authors note that benchmark outcomes depend heavily on the statistical decision rule applied to the genuine multipartite entanglement (GME) threshold (F = 0.5). While all nine devices passed the non-exclusion boundary (Y + ε ≥ 0.5) at some register size, tightening the criterion to strictly certify GME (Y − ε ≥ 0.5) reduced the passing cohort to four systems: IBM Phoenix (up to 16 to 22 qubits), IBM Basquecountry (16 to 22 qubits), AQT IBEX Q1 (12 qubits), and IQM Garnet (8 to 11 qubits).
Comparing hardware specifications against observed fidelities also demonstrated that median calibration metrics are unreliable predictors of multi-qubit performance. On superconducting backends, effective per-qubit GHZ error rates exceeded median two-qubit gate errors by roughly one order of magnitude, highlighting the impact of unmitigated readout errors, idling decoherence, crosstalk, and compiler qubit-chain selection.
