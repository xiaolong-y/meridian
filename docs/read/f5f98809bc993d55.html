Adelaide University Engineers Synthetic Area Spread to Unlock High-Sensitivity Absolute Quantum Magnetometers

Quantum Computing Report Mohamed Abdel-Kareem 1d quantum 557 words 3 min read

Quantum engineers at Adelaide University’s Quantum and Nano Technology Group (QuaNTeG) have resolved a long-standing performance trade-off in superconducting quantum magnetometers, demonstrating a technique called synthetic area spread that enables absolute magnetic field sensing without degrading intrinsic detector sensitivity. Published in Physical Review Applied, the study reveals how two-dimensional Superconducting Quantum Interference Filter (SQIF) arrays can identify absolute zero-field baselines using uniform loop geometries, bypassing the physical lithography challenges and screening distortions historically required for absolute magnetometry.

While conventional DC SQUIDs rank among the most sensitive magnetic field detectors available, their periodic voltage-magnetic flux (VMF) response prevents them from functioning directly as absolute magnetometers. In standard SQIF arrays, engineers generate a unique voltage dip—known as an anti-peak—at zero flux by fabricating loops with incommensurate (mutually non-divisible) physical areas, causing destructive interference across all non-zero fields. However, varying loop sizes drastically alters the inductance (L) of individual cells, destabilizing the critical screening parameter βL and compromising the intrinsic sensitivity of the junctions.
The Adelaide research team—led by Associate Professor Giuseppe Tettamanzi alongside PhD researchers Ross Monaghan and Jonathan Marenkovic—demonstrated that physical area variations are mathematically unnecessary. By reformulating the RSJ equations of motion to incorporate bare superconducting loops (closed loops containing no Josephson junctions), the authors showed that bare loops inject an anomalous inductive flux term into the network. Because Kirchhoff’s current conservation constraints overdetermine the array, this inductive contribution maps onto the remaining active junctions as an effective synthetic area spread. Consequently, a circuit with identical physical loop areas produces the interference signature of an incommensurate array.
The researchers verified the design experimentally by fabricating test arrays through commercial superconductor foundry SEEQC. While standard 16×16 uniform arrays displayed typical periodic VMF oscillations, a 46×16 device—comprising sixteen rows of active Josephson junctions separated by pairs of bare-loop rows—produced a pronounced, reproducible central anti-peak at zero flux. The underlying concept is protected under international patent application PCT/AU2025/050991, establishing a streamlined fabrication methodology for quantum-limited sensors across magnetoencephalography (MEG) brain imaging, low-noise RF receivers, and quantum hardware readout.
