Your Best Quantum Error Correction Engineer Might Be a Bot

Quantum Computing Report Doug Finke 15h quantum 1062 words 6 min read

By Doug Finke
The rate of innovation in quantum computing is accelerating. One of the reasons for this is that quantum providers are now using AI extensively for many of the design and other activities they used to do manually.
At GQI, we categorize the interplay of AI and Quantum into three broad categories:
- AI for Quantum
- Quantum for AI
- Quantum + AI Hybrid Algorithms
Although “Quantum for AI” and the “Quantum + AI” may be a little further out due to their requirements for more powerful quantum computers which aren’t quite available yet, the AI for Quantum is here in spades today.
One area that I would like to highlight in this article is the use of AI to help develop improved Quantum Error Correction (QEC) codes as well as new decoder algorithms that measure the ancilla qubits and figure out what errors have occurred and how to correct the errors. As we’ve discussed many times, one of the keys for achieving commercial quantum advantage is to utilize quantum error correction to make the calculations more accurate and reliable, and error-free.
Over the years, QEC codes have evolved from relatively simple codes such as the surface code or color code to very complex qLDPC (Quantum Low Density Parity Check) codes. In addition, research on QEC codes was performed with previous systems only contained a few dozen or perhaps a few hundred qubits. But soon, QEC codes will be required for systems that will contain thousands of qubits. The increase size greatly increases the complexity of the design.
In the meantime, the requirements placed upon an engineer designing a QEC code keep getting more stringent and sometimes conflicting. At the same time, the engineers want to minimize the logical error rate, decrease the number of physical qubits per logical qubits, and minimize the cycle time it takes to perform a logical gate, error detection, and error correction process. There will be tradeoffs between these three factors and the engineer needs to figure out the best balance between these requirements.
The scope of the problem and the enormous number of possibilities that need to be investigated grows to become more than what an individual can do manually as the quantum processer keep scaling up. This is where the AI assist because so helpful.
Here are a few examples of where AI is being called in to help.
IBM has created an open-source, LLM-guided evolutionary AI framework called OpenEvolve, The framework establishes a powerful, two-way interplay between classical AI and quantum computing. It utilizes large language models (LLMs) to generate informed hypotheses for algebraic expressions that could serve as valid code candidates. A recent test found 465 distinct quantum error correction code candidates. Some of the codes provided the lowest ratio of physical qubits per logical qubits to make maximum use of the available hardware, while others provided the best protection against errors by maximizing the number of errors that could still be corrected. Researchers can then review the different codes to see which one is most appropriate for their particular use case.
QC Design has introduced Meridian, a specialized AI platform designed to automate and optimize fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) hardware architectures. In another test Meridian achieved a 14.6X median reduction in evaluated logical error rates compared to state-of-the-art methods published in scientific literature across an evaluation suite of over 100 fault-tolerance design tasks.
Error correction codes contain both data qubits and ancilla qubits. Since one cannot directly measure a data qubit without destroying the information it contains, the ancilla qubits will contain certain patterns which can indirectly provide information on the states of the data qubits. The ancilla qubits are measured and return a pattern of ones and zeros that can be used to determine if there are any errors in the data qubits. Decoding this pattern is a critical function in fault tolerant quantum computers and is performed by classical electronic components such as FPGAs and GPUs. Ideally, a decoder will always be 100% correct in determining which qubits have errors, but this is never fully achieved, particularly if there are a large number qubit errors that exceed the number of errors the code was designed for. (This design point is called code distance.) Nonetheless, the goal of the error decoder design is to maximize the percentage of instances where it correctly decodes and corrects errors while do so in the shortest period of time.
Google has developed an AI-based decoder called AlphaQubit. This decoder uses a transformer-based neural networks to decode errors in quantum circuits, achieving superior performance compared to existing methods. In tests, AlphaQubit reduced errors by 6% over tensor network decoders and by 30% compared to correlated matching. The system also demonstrated adaptability to larger, simulated quantum devices with up to 241 qubits, maintaining high accuracy across extended error-correction rounds.
NVIDIA has introduced Ising Decoding which supports two variants of a 3D convolutional neural network model that can be optimized for either speed or accuracy to perform real-time decoding for quantum error correction. Ising Decoding models are up to 2.5x faster and 3x more accurate than pyMatching, the current open-source industry standard.
These improvements provide direct benefits to end users. By reducing the number of physical qubits per logical qubits to implement a code, it will allow someone to use smaller and less costly quantum computer to run their program. In addition, those smaller quantum computers will be available earlier than the larger ones.
By improving the error correction capability of the logical qubits, something that can be measured by the logical error rate and as well as by another measure called code distance, the accuracy rate of a user’s calculation will be better and minimize cases where the quantum computer returns the wrong answer.
And finally, improvements in the speed of the quantum error decoding and correction process, will directly result in reductions in the wall clock time needed to complete a user’s program. This can be quite significant for the larger problems that we expect that quantum computers will be used for in the future.
So, while it may be an overstatement to say that complete quantum error correction design and implementation is being performed by a Bot, AI is certain assisting and accelerating the move to implement fault tolerant computers reducing the time it will take to start achieving commercial quantum advantage.
